Exoskeleton idea lab

First-order numbers for sizing an idea. Pick a joint, read how spring-like it is in part of the cycle, then try a spring or a motor and see the torque, power and energy it would have to handle.

How spring-like is the joint?

Moment plotted against angle over the whole cycle. In the highlighted window a straight-line fit gives the joint's quasi-stiffness: the stiffness a spring would need to copy the joint there.

Can a hip sensor tell where you are in the cycle?

Hip angle against hip angular velocity traces a loop once per stride. The angle around that loop is a common way for a controller to estimate gait phase.

These numbers assume the person keeps exactly the same joint angles with the device on. Real users adapt, so use them to size hardware and compare ideas, not to predict metabolic savings.

Where the numbers come from

Every curve is traceable to a peer-reviewed dataset. Anything we derived or assumed is listed here, with the checks we ran on it.

Data and model

  • Joint angles and moments. 24 healthy young adults (14 men, 10 women; 27.6 ± 4.4 years; 68.4 ± 12.2 kg) walked barefoot on an instrumented treadmill at eight speeds, 40% to 145% of their comfortable speed. Means and standard deviations per speed come from the Walking Biomechanics Dataset, WBDS (Fukuchi, Fukuchi & Duarte, 2018), as tabulated in the supplementary spreadsheet of Fukuchi & Duarte (2019).
  • Any speed between the measured ones. The same paper fits, at each 1% of the cycle, a quadratic in dimensionless speed v* = v / √(g·L). The page uses those published coefficients and the published ±1 SD prediction interval. Speed is limited to the range spanned by the eight measured group means, v* 0.17 to 0.62 (0.50 to 1.82 m/s for an 0.88 m leg); the authors give 0.13 to 0.78 as the model's reliable range.
  • Moments are net joint moments divided by body mass, with the source's signs: hip flexor +, knee extensor +, ankle plantarflexor +.
  • Stride time. We fitted dimensionless step frequency to dimensionless speed on 132 treadmill trials from 22 of the same participants (speeds 40–115% of comfortable): f* = 0.905·v*0.510, R² = 0.93. Data: per-trial cadence table in the authors' h2a_age_spt repository. Above v* 0.59 (about 1.73 m/s for an 0.88 m leg) the fit is extrapolated.
  • Joint power (derived). Net moment × joint angular velocity in the sagittal plane, with angular velocity taken from the mean angle curve and the stride time. It is the power of the average pattern, not the average of each person's power.
  • Toe-off (estimated). The first instant after the push-off peak where the mean ankle moment drops below 0.1 N·m/kg.
  • Phases follow Perry & Burnfield (2010). Loading response ends at opposite toe-off (toe-off − 50%, assuming the two legs are symmetric); mid- and terminal stance split single-leg support in half; swing is split into thirds.
  • Animation. Segment lengths use Winter's (2009) body proportions for a 1.72 m person. The source has no pelvis, trunk or arm data, so the trunk is drawn upright, arms are left out, and the whole leg is rotated by one constant angle (about 11°) so the foot is flat in mid-stance. The belt pattern moves with the standing foot. None of this changes any number on the page.
  • Burst labels (H1–H3, K1–K4, A1–A2) follow common usage in the gait literature; Winter (1983) described the ankle and knee bursts across cadences.

Checks we ran

  1. Model vs measured means. At the eight measured speeds the published model reproduces the group means with a root-mean-square error of at most 1.2° (angles) and 0.032 N·m/kg (moments).
  2. Stride and stance timing. Predicted stance time at 0.88, 1.23 and 1.60 m/s is 0.77, 0.63 and 0.54 s. The same young participants walking overground measured 0.79 ± 0.08, 0.63 ± 0.05 and 0.55 ± 0.05 s (da Silva et al., 2020).
  3. Toe-off rule. Applied to the Schwartz et al. (2008) dataset, which reports foot-off, the rule lands within 1.3% of the cycle for four of five speed groups and 2.8% for the slowest.
  4. Power from average curves. The Schwartz et al. (2008) dataset publishes angle, moment and power together. Deriving power from its average angle and moment gives curve shapes that correlate with the published power at r ≥ 0.99 (ankle), ≥ 0.98 (knee) and 0.83–0.97 (hip), and joint shares of positive work within 7 percentage points.
  5. Another lab. At 1.25 m/s this page gives positive-work shares of hip 44%, knee 17%, ankle 39%. Nuckols et al. (2020) report hip 37%, knee 19%, ankle 44% at the same speed. The two disagree on the hip–ankle split by 5–8 percentage points, so treat shares as approximate.

Limits to keep in mind

  • Group averages of young adults on a treadmill. Older adults, overground walking, slopes and shoes change the pattern; individuals differ (see the SD band).
  • Joint power is sagittal-plane only and derived, not measured per person.
  • The lab assumes unchanged kinematics. It does not predict metabolic cost.
